Historically, hospital clinical laboratories held little sway in decision-making processes, but their burgeoning influence in recent times raises questions. This shift can be attributed to the increasingly stringent pharmaceutical regulations that have eroded the profitability of drug sales for hospitals, compelling them to reinforce their diagnostic departments as an alternative revenue stream. This necessity aligns with the serendipitous advent of molecular diagnostics, which has significantly elevated the field's profile. A forward-thinking pathology department would seize this opportunity to bring internally developed testing services, such as genetic assays, under its direct governance. This would include all novel diagnostic technologies derived from pathological specimens. For services that meet regulatory standards, direct billing should be implemented. Where charges are not feasible, collaboration with relevant entities to establish medical billing codes is advised. The most formidable challenge lies with innovations not yet sanctioned by policy; here, influential industry leaders must advocate for regulatory support. Should the pathology department successfully align with industry advancements and facilitate investment, the influx of capital will inevitably attract top-tier talent, laying a robust foundation for the discipline's future over the ensuing decades. However, the current state of affairs paints a different picture: the pathology department appears inert, contributing neither to genetic testing advancements nor to the integration of AI in diagnostic imaging—a technology that is not only relevant but also well-established—resulting in the stagnation of numerous initiatives.

Some may argue that despite understanding the aforementioned dynamics, the pathology sector is uncertain about where to initiate development. The approach, however, is straightforward. For instance, the sector can establish standardized pricing for pathology-related Laboratory Developed Tests (LDTs) and AI diagnostic readings. Additionally, it could advocate through medical associations for dedicated funds, similar to the incentives provided in the domestic new energy vehicle sector, leveraging policy to drive growth in focused areas. Inaction and a reliance on informal incentives are unsustainable. A lack of proactive engagement erodes confidence within the industry, diminishes investment, and ultimately leads to a detrimental cycle, resulting in stagnation.
